## Deadwooding or Full Removal, Which Do You Need?

Three situations get called about a dead or sick-looking tree, and they are not the
same job. **A tree that is mostly healthy with a few dead limbs**, often from age, storm damage or shading out lower branches, usually just needs those limbs removed. That is called deadwooding, and it leaves the rest of the canopy untouched. **A tree with disease spread across a larger portion of the canopy**, southern pine beetle in a loblolly pine or oak wilt in a water oak, is usually a removal candidate once established. Treatment at that stage rarely saves the tree, and the disease can spread to neighbors nearby. **A tree that is already dead standing**, no live foliage anywhere, is a removal job regardless of species. A standing dead tree only gets more hazardous with time, not less. The inspection tells you which of the
three you actually have.

## How Long It Takes

Deadwooding a handful of limbs from an otherwise healthy tree usually takes under two
hours. A full removal of a dead or diseased tree takes about as long as a standard
removal of the same size, since the cutting and rigging work the same either way. A
severely diseased tree with brittle wood can actually take extra care, since dead or
disease-weakened wood can fail unpredictably during the cut rather than behaving like
sound timber. You get a time estimate at the inspection based on the actual tree.

## What Goes Wrong When This Is Done Badly

Cutting into disease-weakened or dead wood without accounting for how brittle it is
can cause a limb or section to fail unpredictably mid-cut. Leaving dead limbs in a
tree that gets called "trimmed" rather than actually removing them back to sound wood
leaves the hazard in place under a fresh-looking canopy. Treating an infested tree,
southern pine beetle in particular, as a wait-and-see problem instead of removing it
promptly lets the infestation spread to healthy trees nearby before anyone acts.
Skipping the inspection and assuming a dead-looking tree is simple to drop can miss
that the trunk itself is hollow or compromised, changing how it needs to come down.

## What a Typical Job Looks Like

A homeowner calls about a loblolly pine with browning needles starting at the top and
small pitch tubes on the bark, a sign of southern pine beetle. The inspection confirms
active infestation and recommends removal rather than treatment, since the infestation
is already established and the tree poses a spread risk to two healthy pines nearby.
The crew removes the tree in sections given its proximity to a fence line, then hauls
the wood off site rather than chipping it, since infested wood should not be left on
the property. Total time on site runs a few hours for a job this size.
